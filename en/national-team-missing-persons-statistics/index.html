The situation

More than 10,000 people in Gaza are missing. Their families still do not know what happened to them

Since October 2023, more than 10,000 people in the Gaza Strip have gone missing.1,2 Most are believed to lie beneath the rubble of destroyed homes. Others were separated from their families during displacement, are presumed detained, or were buried without being identified. Nearly half of the documented cases are women and children.4

Recovery is slow. Most remains have been recovered by hand, because the heavy machinery and forensic equipment needed are not available in sufficient quantity.3

These figures come from different sources, compiled with different methods. They are not one consolidated count. Establishing an authoritative national figure, through the National Reference Register, is one of the first tasks of the National Team.

Who is missing

Under the rubble

People believed buried in destroyed buildings

Separated in displacement

People lost during evacuation and repeated movement

Presumed detained

People whose families believe they were taken and have no information

Buried unidentified

Remains buried in temporary or unmarked graves without identification

Forcibly disappeared

People whose fate and whereabouts are concealed

Families live with ambiguous loss. Without confirmation of death they also face legal uncertainty over guardianship, inheritance, marital status and property, alongside economic hardship.

From report to return

How a missing person is searched for and identified

  1. 01

    Report

    A family reports a missing relative

  2. 02

    Registration

    The case is entered in the National Reference Register

  3. 03

    Family information

    Families give a description, records and, where needed, reference DNA samples

  4. 04

    Site assessment and protection

    Possible locations are identified and protected

  5. 05

    Recovery

    Remains are recovered with care and documented

  6. 06

    Forensic examination

    Examination by forensic specialists

  7. 07

    Identification

    Findings are matched against the register and family information

  8. 08

    Return to the family

    The family is informed and remains are returned for dignified burial

For families

If someone in your family is missing

If someone in your family is missing

  1. Keep any photographs, identity documents, medical and dental records, and descriptions of clothing and personal effects
  2. Note when and where your relative was last seen, and who was with them
  3. Report the case through the official national channel, so it is recorded once in the National Register
  4. If asked, give a reference sample: it will be used only to identify your relative

International cooperation

Working with partners

The Team works with international organisations and with national search mechanisms in other countries to draw on proven practice. It has signed a Memorandum of Understanding with the Committee on Missing Persons in Cyprus. Its methodology also draws on the experience of national search mechanisms in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Colombia and Kosovo, and follows ICRC guidance on the dignified handling of the dead. Partner support is organised around the six subcommittees and channelled through the National Team.
