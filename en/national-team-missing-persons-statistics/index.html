The situation

More than 10,000 people in Gaza are missing. Their families still do not know what happened to them

Since October 2023, more than 10,000 people in the Gaza Strip have gone missing.1,2 Most are believed to lie beneath the rubble of destroyed homes. Others were separated from their families during displacement, are presumed detained, or were buried without being identified. Nearly half of the documented cases are women and children.4

Recovery is slow. Most remains have been recovered by hand, because the heavy machinery and forensic equipment needed are not available in sufficient quantity.3

Families live with ambiguous loss. Without confirmation of death they also face legal uncertainty over guardianship, inheritance, marital status and property, alongside economic hardship.

Why cases remain open

Why are so many cases still unresolved?

Establishing the fate of the missing in Gaza is an immense humanitarian task. Eight factors, often in combination, keep cases open and leave families without answers.

01 / 08

Buried beneath the rubble

Most of the missing are believed to lie beneath destroyed homes, shelters and public buildings. The scale of destruction delays recovery and the identification of victims.

ExampleAt a single site in Az-Zeitoun, Gaza City, Civil Defence teams recovered 96 bodies and remains in September 2026, including 58 children and 23 women.3
02 / 08

Restricted access to sites

Restrictions on access cover about 65 per cent of the Gaza Strip.5 Many places where people are believed buried cannot be reached by search and recovery teams, so remains stay unrecovered for long periods.

ExampleIn Shuja'iyya, Gaza City, a woman lost her husband and 16-year-old son in October 2023. A military position now stands on the site where they are believed to lie.7
03 / 08

No heavy equipment, no safe recovery capacity

Entry of heavy machinery for rubble removal has been refused, and most remains have been recovered by hand.3 Many families have become search teams themselves, working through unstable rubble at risk to their own safety.

ExampleMore than two years after his home was destroyed, a father in Gaza City was still sifting its rubble with a sieve, searching for the remains of his wife and children.8
04 / 08

Clearance before documentation

When rubble is levelled or removed before forensic examination, human remains, personal belongings and the information needed to identify the dead can be lost for good.

ExampleIn Rafah, a family with 14 members still missing found that the site of their home had been levelled and built over by August 2026.7
05 / 08

Emergency burials, unmarked graves and damaged cemeteries

Many of the dead were buried in haste, in temporary or mass graves, without records. Cemeteries have also been disturbed, so families cannot find graves and remains are harder to identify.

ExampleFamilies have been unable to locate their relatives' graves in Al-Batsh Cemetery, Gaza City, after it was excavated with bulldozers in January 2026.9
06 / 08

Limited identification capacity

DNA testing facilities do not currently exist in Gaza,10 and the entry of DNA kits has been restricted.11 Thousands of remains cannot yet be matched to a name.

ExampleFamilies gather in hospital halls to view photographs of returned, unidentified bodies, hoping to recognise a relative from clothing or personal effects.12
07 / 08

Undisclosed detention and enforced disappearance

Many families do not know whether a missing relative has been detained, displaced or killed. ICRC visits to places of detention remain suspended,13 leaving relatives with no information about the fate and whereabouts of their loved ones.

ExampleA man from Khan Younis left home for afternoon prayer in October 2023 and never returned. Years of inquiries by his family have produced no answer.14
08 / 08

Communication blackouts and lost records

The collapse of communications and records systems has made it difficult for families, authorities and humanitarian organisations to register cases, share information and coordinate searches.

ExampleA young woman in Gaza City searches every day for her 17-year-old brother, posting his photograph across the city.15

Together, these factors delay families' right to know. They are also why the National Reference Register comes first: information gathered from families now is what will make identification possible later.

The national response

The National Team for the Follow-Up of the Missing Persons File

The National Team is the competent national authority and the single national reference point for the missing persons file. Its mandate is humanitarian: to establish the fate and whereabouts of the missing and to support their families.

Established
Council of Ministers Decision No. 19/95/01 of 27 January 202616
Chair
H.E. the Minister of Justice, with the Ministry of Justice as Rapporteur
Membership
Line ministries, the judiciary, national institutions and civil society
Mandate
Humanitarian: the fate and whereabouts of the missing, and support to their families

Governing principle

Information first, forensics last.

The National Reference Register and its platform come first. Field and laboratory work depend on reliable information from families.

From report to return

How a missing person is searched for and identified

  1. 01

    Report

    A family reports a missing relative

  2. 02

    Registration

    The case is entered in the National Reference Register

  3. 03

    Family information

    Families give a description, records and, where needed, reference DNA samples

  4. 04

    Site assessment and protection

    Possible locations are identified and protected

  5. 05

    Recovery

    Remains are recovered with care and documented

  6. 06

    Forensic examination

    Examination by forensic specialists

  7. 07

    Identification

    Findings are matched against the register and family information

  8. 08

    Return to the family

    The family is informed and remains are returned for dignified burial

For families

If someone in your family is missing

If someone in your family is missing

  1. Keep any photographs, identity documents, medical and dental records, and descriptions of clothing and personal effects
  2. Note when and where your relative was last seen, and who was with them
  3. Report the case through the official national channel, so it is recorded once in the National Register
  4. If asked, give a reference sample: it will be used only to identify your relative

International cooperation

Working with partners

The Team works with international organisations and with national search mechanisms in other countries to draw on proven practice. It has signed a Memorandum of Understanding with the Committee on Missing Persons in Cyprus. Its methodology also draws on the experience of national search mechanisms in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Colombia and Kosovo, and follows ICRC guidance on the dignified handling of the dead. Partner support is organised around the six subcommittees and channelled through the National Team.
